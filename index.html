You already know the feeling. You wake up tired before the day has asked anything of you yet. You tell yourself it's the traffic, the workload, the kids, the harmattan. But somewhere quieter, you wonder if it's something else.

By 2pm, your body wants sugar the way a phone wants power. Maybe it's a chilled Coke from the office fridge, maybe it's biscuits from the woman by the gate. You don't even enjoy it the way you used to. You just need it to get through the afternoon.

At owambe, or just hanging out on a Friday, you catch yourself doing a quiet calculation nobody else at the table seems to be doing. Small chops — is that okay? One more chicken lap — will that matter tonight? You smile and eat anyway, because saying no out loud feels like admitting something you're not ready to explain.

Somewhere in your phone gallery is a hospital result you screenshotted and never showed anyone. "Fatty liver," or "elevated liver enzymes," typed in that flat clinical font that doesn't care that your hands were a little unsteady when you first read it.

And the advice you got was "eat clean" and "cut down on sugar" — from a doctor with four more patients waiting and no time to explain what that actually means for someone whose lunch options are jollof, amala, or suya. So you nodded, said thank you, and went right back to not knowing what to do.

Here's What Nobody Told You

Fatty liver isn't really a willpower problem. It's a pattern your liver has been quietly absorbing for years, one soft drink and one plate of fried rice at a time.

Your liver's job is to process the fat and sugar that come from what you eat. When your meals are consistently high in added sugar, refined carbohydrates, fried food, and alcohol, your liver simply can't clear it all fast enough. So it starts storing the extra fat, right there in its own tissue. Over time, that's what shows up on a scan as "fatty liver."

In everyday Nigerian life, that pattern rarely looks dramatic. It looks like soft drinks and sweetened juice with almost every meal. White bread and meat pie for breakfast. Instant noodles when you're in a hurry. Fried plantain, fried rice, and fried everything, several times a week. Suya and shawarma as a regular treat instead of an occasional one. None of it feels like "the problem" in the moment — it just feels like normal Nigerian eating.

The fatigue, the belly fat, the constant sugar cravings — they aren't separate issues. They're different symptoms of the same underlying pattern.

This is also why "just eat clean" fails so many of us. It's foreign advice, built around foreign food, handed to people whose culture, budget, and kitchens don't match it. The real fix was never about abandoning Nigerian food. It's about changing which Nigerian foods you lean on most often, how they're cooked, and how they're combined — so your liver gets a break instead of another load to carry.

That one shift in understanding is what changed everything for the man who eventually built this plan.

My Story

My name is Bamidele. I'm thirty-nine, I live in Lagos, and until three years ago, I genuinely thought fatty liver was something that happened to other people — older men who drank heavily, not someone like me who just liked his food and worked hard.

I grew up in Ibadan, the last of four children, and food in our house was love. My mother's efo riro, her ewa agoyin, the smell of her frying akara on Saturday mornings — that was comfort, that was home. When I moved to Lagos for work in my late twenties, I carried that same relationship with food into a very different lifestyle: long hours at a logistics company, hardly any real exercise, and a lot of stress-eating I didn't even recognize as stress-eating.

By my mid-thirties, I had put on close to eighteen kilograms. My belbelation — my belly, if we're being honest — had become the kind of thing my friends joked about at every gathering. "Bamidele, see as you don blow o," they'd say, laughing, and I'd laugh too, because what else could I do. But underneath the laughing, something in me had started to worry.

The tiredness came first. Not the ordinary tiredness of a long day, but a heavy, dragging exhaustion that sleep didn't seem to fix. I'd wake up on a Saturday morning already feeling like I needed another eight hours. My wife, Bukola, noticed before I did. "You're always tired these days," she said one evening, watching me fall asleep on the couch at 8pm again. I brushed it off. I was busy. Every Nigerian man my age was tired, I told myself.

Then came the things I couldn't laugh off. I stopped wanting to be in photos. At my nephew's wedding in Ibadan, I found every excuse to stand at the back or hold the camera instead. My son, Dara, plays football on Saturday mornings, and there was a stretch of about two months where I simply didn't have the energy to go and watch him — I told him I had work, but the truth was I was exhausted and slightly ashamed of how I looked standing on the sidelines. He didn't complain. He just stopped asking if I was coming. That quiet stopped-asking did something to me that no lecture from a doctor ever could.

Before I found what actually worked, I tried a lot of things. I want to be specific about this, because I think the failures matter as much as the solution.

The first thing I tried was "No Carbs After 6pm," a rule I picked up from a fitness page on Instagram. It sounded simple enough. But in a house where family dinner — amala and ewedu, usually — happens around 8pm because everyone is coming from work or school, the rule made no sense. I'd either skip dinner and eat in secret later, or eat dinner and feel like a failure. Within three weeks I'd abandoned it completely, and I think I'd actually gained weight from the stress-snacking that followed.

Then I tried keto. I ordered imported keto snack boxes off Jumia — nut bars, some strange keto bread substitute, "bulletproof coffee" sachets — and spent close to forty thousand naira over two months. My stomach never agreed with half of it. I was bloated, irritable, and honestly miserable at family functions, sitting there explaining why I couldn't eat the rice while everyone else enjoyed themselves. I gave up before the second bag was finished.

After that came extreme intermittent fasting — skipping breakfast and most of lunch, eating one large meal at night. A colleague swore by it. For me, it meant a splitting headache by 11am most days and, once, an embarrassing moment where I nearly fainted in a meeting in Victoria Island. My manager asked if I was alright in front of the whole team. I said I hadn't slept well. I had not eaten properly in almost eighteen hours.

I also fell for a "detox tea" sold by a vendor I found on Instagram, promising to "flush the liver" in fourteen days. It cost fifteen thousand naira for a month's supply. What it actually did was send me to the toilet constantly and leave me weaker, not healthier. No real change to my weight, my energy, or anything that mattered. Just an empty tin and a lesson about how good marketing can be.

And finally, there was the photocopied diet sheet a hospital dietician handed me during one visit — a generic printout full of phrases like "reduce saturated fat" and "limit simple sugars," with example meals like grilled chicken breast, brown rice, and steamed broccoli. Good advice, technically. Completely disconnected from anything in my kitchen, my mother's recipes, or the food stalls near my office. I folded it, put it in a drawer, and never looked at it again.

By the time I turned thirty-six, my father had already been managing type 2 diabetes for almost a decade, and it was always somewhere in the back of my mind that I might be heading the same way, just with a different name for it.

The turning point came on an ordinary Tuesday. I'd gone for a routine check at a clinic in Yaba after weeks of Bukola practically forcing me to book the appointment. The doctor, a calm but direct woman named Dr. Ogunleye, went through my blood work and the ultrasound results with me line by line. My liver enzymes were elevated. The scan showed fat accumulation she described as "moderate" — what she called grade two fatty liver.

She didn't shout or scare-monger. She was almost gentle about it, which somehow made it land harder. "This is still reversible at this stage," she said, "but if the pattern continues, especially with your family history, we could be having a very different conversation in five years. I'm not talking about fatty liver anymore. I'm talking about your liver, your heart, possibly your pancreas too."

“This is still reversible at this stage.”Dr. Ogunleye, Yaba

I sat in my car outside that clinic for almost twenty minutes before I could drive. I called Bukola. I don't remember every word of that call, but I remember my voice cracking somewhere in the middle of it, and I remember her going quiet on the other end before saying, softly, "Okay. So what do we do now. We'll figure it out together."

That night, I thought about Dara, standing at the edge of the football pitch, no longer bothering to ask if I was coming. I thought about the photos I'd been avoiding. I thought about my father's insulin pens in the fridge at my parents' house in Ibadan. And I decided, quietly and without any big announcement, that I was going to figure this out properly — not another borrowed foreign diet, not another Instagram product, but something built around the food I actually ate.

I spent the next several weeks doing what I probably should have done from the start. I read everything I could find on nutrition and liver health from credible sources. I spoke to a friend from university, Chidinma, who had trained as a dietitian and was generous enough to sit with me over several phone calls, sanity-checking what I was learning. I went back to my mother's kitchen in Ibadan on a few weekends, not to eat, but to actually watch and ask questions — how much palm oil she really used, how often we genuinely ate fried food growing up versus how I remembered it, which of our "everyday" dishes were naturally liver-friendly and which ones had quietly become too frequent in my adult life.

What I found surprised me. The problem was never Nigerian food itself. Dishes like efo riro, ewa agoyin, moin moin, and pepper soup, cooked with a lighter hand on the oil, were genuinely good choices. Beans, ugu, ewedu, okra, and unripe plantain were quietly excellent for exactly the kind of liver support I needed. The real issue was the pattern around those foods — how often I was frying instead of boiling or grilling, how much sugar I was drinking without noticing, how little fibre and vegetables were making it onto my plate compared to refined carbs.

I built a simple structure for myself: specific breakfasts, lunches, and dinners for each day, using foods I could actually buy at my regular market, cooked in ways that reduced the load on my liver rather than adding to it. I tracked how I felt — energy, sleep, cravings, my waistline — in a plain notebook, nothing fancy. I gave myself ninety days and treated it like a genuine experiment, not a punishment.

The changes were slower than I wanted at first, and more real than I expected by the end. When I went back to Dr. Ogunleye for a follow-up, my liver enzymes had come down significantly, and the repeat scan showed real improvement. She actually smiled at me that day, which felt like a bigger reward than the numbers themselves.

I didn't set out to build a product. I started sharing my meal templates with two friends who'd seen my own results and started asking questions — "Bamidele, wetin you dey chop sef, you don change." Then it was five people. Then it was people I didn't know, forwarded through WhatsApp groups. Eventually I sat down and organised everything properly — the meals, the swaps, the shopping lists — into the structured, no-stress 14-day plan you're looking at right now.

This is the plan I wish someone had handed me on that Tuesday outside the clinic in Yaba — instead of a photocopied sheet I never used.
